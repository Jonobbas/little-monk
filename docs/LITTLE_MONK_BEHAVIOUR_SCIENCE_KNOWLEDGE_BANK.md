## 1. Purpose

Behavioural change for good is the foundation of Little Monk. This evidence and methods bank supports the agent when a user chooses a personal goal, routine, reflection, or behaviour. It helps the agent understand possible influences on action, offer suitable support, and explain uncertainty. The user defines the direction and what improvement means to them.

It is a distinct but complementary source to the Japanese Principles bank:

| Bank | Main question | What it can contribute | What it cannot establish by itself |
|---|---|---|---|
| Behaviour Science | What may help or hinder this person’s chosen action in this situation? | Behavioural mechanisms, studied intervention components, design methods, outcomes, and limitations | That a particular practice will work for this user or that Little Monk is effective |
| Japanese Principles | What meanings, perspectives, and practices can invite reflection? | Culturally situated philosophy, literature, aesthetics, traditions, and interpretation | Clinical efficacy or universal claims about Japanese people |

Research may later show useful connections between the banks. Keep the source and claim type visible when making those connections. The Japanese-content percentage gate applies only to the Japanese-principles collection; it does not apply to behaviour-science sources.



## Product philosophy: user-directed continuous improvement

**Product motto:** “Japanese Wisdom for Behavioural Change — Personal Growth, Your Way.”

Behavioural change for good is Little Monk’s foundation. The app supports positive change that can continue and build over time, guided by the person’s own direction and priorities. Kaizen contributes an inspiration for continuous improvement; behaviour science informs how the agent can support action, reflection, habits, feedback, and adaptation. The user defines what “good” means and which areas of life matter. Little Monk does not prescribe one ideal life or promise that every area will improve at once. Progress can change pace, and the user remains in control.

## 2. Scope for the first version

Prioritize research that helps the agent support the user through a humane, non-prescriptive conversation:

1. **Understand the situation:** distinguish ability or knowledge, opportunity and context, motivation, competing demands, and the user’s own reasons.
2. **Support user-owned goals:** clarify what the user wants, why it matters to them, and what level of change feels acceptable.
3. **Turn intention into a feasible next step:** consider action planning, obstacle planning, cues, and a small first action where the user chooses it.
4. **Support repetition and adaptation:** understand habit formation, monitoring, feedback, lapses, and maintenance without imposing a fixed timeline or treating a lapse as failure.
5. **Protect emotional safety and agency:** use respectful language; avoid shame, coercion, manipulative nudges, and unsupported mental-health or medical claims.
6. **Evaluate the product:** distinguish usability, acceptability, engagement, behaviour outcomes, wellbeing outcomes, and harms. Treat research on AI chatbots and conversational agents as an important delivery-method evidence stream: ask which users, behaviours, mechanisms, outcomes, and follow-up periods have actually been studied. Do not treat satisfaction or continued app use as proof of benefit.

This is not a diagnostic framework. The agent must not infer a person’s character, mental-health status, or future behaviour from a short conversation. A model such as COM-B is a set of prompts for considering context, not a label to attach to the user.

## 3. Evidence handling rules

- A theory explains or organizes possible mechanisms; it does not prove that an intervention works.
- A taxonomy helps describe intervention components; inclusion in the taxonomy does not mean a component is effective.
- Findings are bounded by the studied population, context, intervention, comparator, outcome, and follow-up period.
- Record null, mixed, and adverse findings alongside positive findings. Prefer systematic reviews and direct studies, but check their included evidence and limitations.
- Preserve the authors’ findings separately from Little Monk’s design interpretation. Label an interpretation as a hypothesis until tested.
- Do not use health or clinical findings as a direct promise about general life outcomes. Do not imply that behaviour science validates the Japanese-principles bank as a whole.
- User feedback can guide that user’s experience. It becomes a broader product insight only when consented, de-identified, contextualized, and reviewed; it is not automatically research evidence.
- No source becomes approved agent knowledge until it passes the existing [Admission Gates](LITTLE_MONK_KNOWLEDGE_BANK_ADMISSION_GATES.md), has a complete record in the [Source Register](LITTLE_MONK_KNOWLEDGE_BANK_REGISTER.md), and receives a permanent research ID.

### Evidence labels

| Label | Meaning | Permitted use |
|---|---|---|
| Framework / theory | A proposed model or explanatory account | Organize questions and generate hypotheses; do not claim effectiveness from the model alone |
| Taxonomy / method | A way to classify or deliver intervention components | Describe what an intervention contains; effectiveness needs separate evidence |
| Systematic review / meta-analysis | A structured synthesis of defined studies | Summarize the included evidence while recording quality, heterogeneity, and scope |
| Controlled intervention | A randomized or other controlled test | Make appropriately bounded claims about the tested intervention and sample |
| Observational / qualitative | Association, experience, or implementation evidence | Describe patterns and lived experience; do not infer causation or prevalence beyond the method |
| Design / feasibility | Development, usability, acceptability, or implementation work | Inform product design and research questions; do not present feasibility as efficacy |

## 4. Agent use pattern

The following is a design hypothesis informed by the candidate literature, not a validated Little Monk protocol. It operationalizes the motto by helping users pursue their own chosen, positive changes over time:

1. **Ask permission and clarify:** “What would you like to change or understand?” Ask about constraints only when relevant.
2. **Explore before advising:** ask whether the main difficulty feels like time, energy, knowledge, access, confidence, competing priorities, or something else. Let the user correct the framing.
3. **Offer choices:** if the user wants ideas, present one or two options with a brief reason, uncertainty, and an easy way to decline or adapt them.
4. **Plan only by invitation:** the user may choose a small action, a cue, or an optional if–then plan. Do not turn a wish into an obligation or schedule without consent.
5. **Check in by consent:** ask whether follow-up would be welcome and how often. At review, ask what happened and what the user learned; avoid grades, streak pressure, or moral judgments.
6. **Adapt:** if the plan did not happen, ask what got in the way and whether to change, pause, or stop. Do not presume lack of willpower.
7. **Keep memory separate:** save a preference or plan only with the user’s understanding and consent. Never convert a personal journal entry into shared knowledge.

## 6. Initial learning sequence

For the first review cycle, read in this order:

1. COM-B / Behaviour Change Wheel — a way to think about context before proposing an action.
2. Self-Determination Theory intervention meta-analysis — motivation and competence as candidate mechanisms.
3. Mental contrasting with implementation intentions — connecting a desired future with a concrete obstacle and plan.
4. Progress-monitoring meta-analysis — when feedback or recording can support action, plus the privacy and pressure trade-offs.
5. Habit-formation review — repetition and variability without a fixed timeline promise.
6. Behaviour-change technique taxonomy — precise documentation of intervention ingredients.
7. Self-compassion and conversational-agent reviews — tone, delivery, and boundaries.

The order is a practical reading plan, not a ranking of evidence quality or importance.
