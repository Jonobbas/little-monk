# Little Monk — Japanese Books and Related Reading Bibliography

**Prepared:** 10 October 2026  
**Scope:** A substantial, curated bibliography for Little Monk’s Japanese-principle-informed reflection and habit-support model. This is not literally every Japanese book: that would be an unbounded national bibliography. It prioritizes relevant Japanese-authored primary works, Japanese classics, scholarship, and clearly labeled modern interpretations. Bibliographic pages were checked against publishers, national/library catalogs, or academic presses where available.

## How to use this list

A book can inspire a reflective prompt or practice, but a book alone does not establish that a method is effective for every person. Keep three records distinct:

1. **Cultural/philosophical source** — what the author or tradition actually says, with context.
2. **Little Monk interpretation** — the specific, modest product idea we derive from it.
3. **Empirical evidence** — separately reviewed studies that test an intervention or outcome.

Do not cite a popular introduction as proof of a medical, psychological, or causal claim. Where a source is in Japanese, record the edition/translation and have a competent reader check nuances before turning it into a product card. This list is an initial reading map; full-text review and source cards remain to be completed.

## D. Concepts to source before writing product cards

For these topics, the register should add specific scholarly or primary references before Little Monk makes a claim or uses a term as a practice label:

- **Hansei (反省):** reflective review in specific educational, organizational, or personal contexts; distinguish ordinary reflection from imported management interpretations.
- **Shoshin (初心):** beginner’s mind; read Zen sources in context, including Suzuki, and avoid turning it into a simple productivity slogan.
- **Wabi-sabi (侘寂):** investigate Japanese tea, craft, and aesthetics sources alongside modern interpreters.
- **Mono no aware (物の哀れ):** use Japanese literary scholarship and original texts; do not reduce it to “mindfulness of impermanence.”
- **Omoiyari (思いやり), wa (和), and mottainai (もったいない):** investigate changing everyday, social, and historical uses before designing prompts.
- **Kaizen and hansei in Toyota/industry:** use Japanese-language primary management texts and peer-reviewed organizational research, separately from self-help adaptations.
- **Ikigai:** put Mieko Kamiya and Japanese-language scholarship ahead of viral diagrams and wellness claims.

## Suggested source-review order

1. Obtain *Ikigai ni tsuite* and confirm whether the team can review Japanese text or locate a credible translation.
2. Read Ken Mogi as a Japanese author’s contemporary popular interpretation and compare with Kamiya.
3. Read the *Ma* essay collection and choose only contributors whose claims can be accurately attributed.
4. Read Suzuki and Masuno for Zen practice language; mark them as religious/philosophical sources.
5. Read Miyazaki and inspect the underlying empirical papers before any health-related wording.
6. Use Okakura, Yanagi, Tanizaki, and Kenkō for aesthetic/literary context.
7. Use Imai as a source for process adaptation, not individual wellbeing.
8. Use the scholarly philosophy reference to check cultural and historical context.
9. Review popular modern interpreters comparatively and record where their claims go beyond primary texts.

## Source notes and limitations

- This catalogue is curated for Little Monk’s current themes, not a complete list of Japanese publishing.
- “Japanese author” describes authorship, not automatically expertise in every topic discussed.
- Publisher descriptions verify bibliographic facts and intended scope; they are not independent validation of a book’s claims.
- Bibliographic verification is not the same as full-text review. Each book remains queued until reviewed and summarized with page-level/section references.
- Use short paraphrases in the knowledge bank. Do not copy full copyrighted books or substantial passages into prompts, vector stores, or product datasets without rights.
- Keep any participant experience separate from book-derived material and from research evidence.
