# Little Monk Knowledge Bank — Source Register and Review Method

**Status:** Initial register and operating method, 10 October 2026  
**Purpose:** Build a source-traceable shared knowledge base for Little Monk’s Japanese-principle-informed reflection and behavior-support model. This is a review queue, not an endorsement of every listed source or a claim that Little Monk is effective.

## How the bank is used

The bank stores shared source notes and practice cards. Each card should connect a bounded principle to its source, context, limitations, and a safe way to offer it. The agent may retrieve a card relevant to a user’s expressed goal and situation, then explain why it might fit. Personal fit remains a tentative hypothesis that the user can accept, change, or reject.

Keep the shared bank separate from private participant memory. Never put diary entries, identifiers, or raw participant stories in this public register. Any future aggregate learning from participant feedback needs explicit consent, de-identification, context, human review, and a clear distinction from published research.

## Evidence and source classes

| Class | Source type | Appropriate use | Do not claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Peer-reviewed systematic reviews, meta-analyses, and robust empirical studies | Bounded claims about studied populations, interventions, outcomes, and uncertainty | That results automatically apply to every person or prove Little Monk works |
| B | Other empirical studies, implementation research, and technical evaluations | Design hypotheses and context-specific findings; note study quality and limits | Broad causal or clinical conclusions beyond the methods |
| C | Japanese philosophical/traditional concepts, books, and practitioner writing | Reflection prompts, language, and practice inspiration with cultural/contextual care | Clinical evidence or a single timeless “Japanese” system |
| D | Participant stories, interviews, and practitioner experience | User-centered design insights and candidate patterns for further review | Population-level evidence or generalizable predictions |

A source can be useful without being strong evidence. Preserve source type and quality labels in all summaries.

## Source record fields

For every item capture: stable ID; full title; author(s)/organization; year/date; source type and evidence class; publication/venue; DOI, PMID, ISBN, or URL; access date; rights/access notes; population and setting (if applicable); study/design or author context; concise paraphrased finding/principle; limitations and conflicts; relevant Little Monk practice tags; safety considerations; reviewer; review date; and status (queued, metadata checked, full source reviewed, synthesized, approved for a practice card, or excluded).

For each practice card, also record the exact source links, intended situation, user choice point, recommended prompt/action, why it may fit, alternatives, contraindications or caution, and a simple outcome question. Keep cards short and traceable. When sources disagree, show the disagreement and context.

## Individualized workflow

1. Ask what the person wants help with and what constraints or preferences matter to them.
2. Retrieve a small number of relevant, reviewed practice cards and separately retrieve only approved memories belonging to that user.
3. Offer one or a few options with the source basis and a plain-language rationale; state uncertainty and ask what feels suitable.
4. Let the user choose, adapt, or decline. Save an action only after confirmation.
5. Ask what happened and how useful the suggestion felt. Treat this as one person’s experience.
6. Update only that user’s approved memory. Add de-identified, consented feedback to a separate candidate-pattern queue for human review; do not silently turn it into shared “evidence.”

Avoid deterministic claims such as predicting how a person will behave. At most, say that a practice may fit based on the person’s own stated preferences and previous responses.

## Japanese principles and books: collection queue

The following are **topic prompts, not validated source entries**. Add specific primary or scholarly sources before writing knowledge cards. Review context, translation, historical period, and whether a contemporary product interpretation overstates or flattens the concept.

- Ma (間): space, interval, or pause in relevant aesthetic/design contexts.
- Kaizen (改善): improvement practices and their organizational/historical contexts.
- Ikigai (生きがい): meanings and uses across Japanese sources, avoiding simplified internet definitions.
- Hansei (反省): reflection and review in the contexts where the term is used.
- Shoshin (初心): beginner’s mind in relevant Buddhist/Zen sources and later adaptations.
- Mushin (無心): relevant philosophical/arts contexts; do not treat as an instruction to suppress emotion.
- Wabi-sabi (侘寂): aesthetic and historical contexts, not a generic wellness claim.
- Related books, academic work, practitioner accounts, and first-person experience should be logged as separate source types.

## Review checklist

Before approving a card:

- Confirm author, title, date, identifier, source type, and stable link against the publisher, journal, catalog, or primary text.
- Read the source itself or a legitimate accessible abstract; do not rely solely on a search result or another site’s summary.
- Record methods, population/context, outcome measured, limitations, and uncertainty.
- Separate the author’s conclusion from Little Monk’s design interpretation.
- Check whether the interpretation fits the source’s cultural and historical context.
- Use original paraphrase and citation. Do not store or redistribute full copyrighted books or articles without rights.
- Have a second reviewer check claims that might affect health, safety, or sensitive personal decisions.
- Test the card in a user journey for clarity, choice, and an easy way to decline or correct the suggestion.
